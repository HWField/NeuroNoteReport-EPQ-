\subsection*{Overview:}
With a note-mapping system researched, designed, and a prototype built, I decided to dedicate this week to exploring various note-taking methods, including both typing and handwriting, to expand on my initial research into effective note-taking strategies.

\subsection*{Achievements, Thoughts and Ideas:}
I approached this week having a strong idea for what I wanted to (and I knew was possible to) build as a tool to present my research I knew it was now crucial that I fully understood what I wanted to achieve and the thought patterns I wanted to address to achieve this. 

So far my understanding of note-taking methods was sourced primarily from my research of techniques and expanded by my own personal findings and theorised best practises, however I hadn't yet explored the vast depth of research completed with more scientific approaches. I utilised JSTOR within the school library to find some fascinating articles which I could learn from:

Firstly I read back over my past notes and revisited  my requirement for handwritten notes to be supported by the software. I had aways felt that longhand forms of data recording aided my ability to recall information (as Is crucial for those in the education system) but I wanted to verify this feeling. I came across \nameref{research:WritingVsTyping} (later built on by \nameref{research:WritingVsTyping2}) which used a large data set of students to confirm my predictions. This week i chose to handwrite my research notes to fit with my findings and ensure I was able to apply my learnings to my own work

I wanted to try and find scientific confirmation that my 'mind map' centred philosophy regarding the storage of information was in fact an ideal solution, whilst also being careful to avoid confirmation bias. This article is a much less formal document however gives a more "grounded" insight from a group of teachers who discuss the impacts of such note taking techniques on their students
Having completed this weeks research pieces (\nameref{research:WritingVsTyping}, \nameref{research:WritingVsTyping2}, \nameref{research:StructuredNoteTaking}, \nameref{research:NotetakingReview},\nameref{research:StrategicNotetaking}, \nameref{research:LaptopVsLonghandSummary}) I have come away with some conclusions. It has been unanimously agreed by all that laptop (typed) notetakers copy lecture / course content far more directly and record more information, while this is beneficial for creating thorough notes this does skip the encoding step of note taking, this step causes the brain to rephrase / reframe ideas to simplify recording by longhand (a much slower recording technique at average 28WPM vs typists around 60). While less detail is recorded and notes are harder to later reference it seems that this encoding effect can contribute to huge academic performance gains making longhand notetaking a more beneficial strategy for students
